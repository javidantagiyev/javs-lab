## A Model That Looks Great — Until It Meets Reality

In <Link href="/learn/ml/how-models-learn">Lesson 04</Link>, we trained a prediction model and watched its error get smaller. But how do we know whether the model **learned a useful pattern**, rather than merely adapting to the examples it was given?

Imagine studying for an exam by memorizing the answers to one practice sheet. You might get every question on that sheet right—but that says surprisingly little about your performance on a new exam.

Machine learning has the same problem. A model evaluated on the very data it learned from can appear better than it truly is. To test whether learning **generalizes**, we reserve examples the model has not seen while fitting its parameters.

Géron introduces *testing and validating* in Chapter 1 (pp. 34–36), then develops practical dataset splitting and model evaluation in Chapter 2. In this lesson, we'll turn those ideas into a hands-on experiment.

## The Three Roles of Data

For a standard supervised-learning workflow, each example has **features** (inputs) and a known **target** (the correct answer). We divide the available examples into non-overlapping groups with different responsibilities:

| Set | Purpose | Can it influence the final model? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Training** | Fit the model's parameters—such as regression coefficients | **Yes.** This is what it learns from. |
| **Validation** | Compare candidate models and choose hyperparameters | **Indirectly.** Our choices depend on its results. |
| **Test** | Estimate performance of the already-chosen model on unseen cases | **No.** Keep it untouched until the end. |

The percentages are flexible. A **60 / 20 / 20** split means 60% of examples are used for training, 20% for validation, and 20% for testing. You can try other ratios in the lab. There is no universally optimal split: dataset size and problem structure matter.

### Why isn't training error enough?

A sufficiently flexible model might memorize noise in the training data. Its training error could be tiny while its predictions on new examples are poor. Validation data gives us a more independent signal while we compare models.

However, after we repeatedly make decisions using validation results, those decisions themselves become tuned to that validation set. **That is why the final test set must remain separate.**

## Interactive Experiment — Split. Select. Test.

The right panel contains **60 invented observations**. Each observation pairs a numerical input with a noisy target value. There is a nonlinear pattern underneath the noise, but you don't know its exact shape.

The lab fits polynomial regression models of five different degrees. Its fitted curves and RMSE values are computed **in your browser** from the data—not generated animations or fixed answers.

### Mission 01 — Split the data

Start with **60 / 20 / 20**. Observe the counts:

- **Training:** 36 examples. These fit the model's coefficients.
- **Validation:** 12 examples. These help you select the polynomial degree.
- **Test:** 12 examples. These remain concealed until you finish selecting.

Try **70 / 15 / 15** and **40 / 30 / 30**. How many examples are now available to train the model? How many remain to evaluate it?

Press **Reshuffle split**. The total dataset stays the same, but different observations become training, validation, and test examples. Notice that evaluation outcomes can depend on which examples end up in each partition.

### Mission 02 — Train several candidates

Click **Train 5 candidates**. For each polynomial degree, the lab fits coefficients using the training examples. You will see two scores:

**Training RMSE** measures prediction error on the examples used to fit the model. **Validation RMSE** measures error on examples the fitting algorithm did not use.

The root mean squared error is:

$$
\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{y}_i-y_i)^2}
$$

Here, **ŷ** is a prediction, **y** is the actual target, and **n** is the number of evaluated examples. Lower RMSE generally means a better fit *on that dataset*, and the unit matches the target's unit.

Select different **degrees** in the candidate table. The pale gold curve changes. A degree-1 model is a straight line; higher degrees allow more complex shapes. Don't assume that the lowest **training** RMSE is the best choice.

### Mission 03 — Choose using validation

Look for the candidate with the **lowest validation RMSE**. You can pick it manually or press **Choose lowest validation error**. That is a simple form of **model selection**.

Before selecting, ask yourself:

1. Which degree has the smallest training error?
2. Which degree has the smallest validation error?
3. Are these necessarily the same degree?

Polynomial degree is a **hyperparameter**: it controls the model family you choose before fitting its coefficients. The fitted coefficients are **model parameters** learned from training data.

### Mission 04 — See what leakage does

Enable **Demonstrate data leakage**, then retrain the candidates. In this intentionally incorrect mode, the algorithm uses the validation examples **during training** and still reports a "validation" error.

That score is now contaminated: the examples are no longer independent of fitting. A low error may look convincing, but we have broken the evaluation procedure. Turn leakage **off** and retrain before evaluating on the final test set.

This is just one form of leakage. Real projects also suffer from near-duplicate records across splits, preprocessing learned from all data, or future information accidentally appearing in today's features.

### Mission 05 — Open the sealed test

Choose your final model using legitimate validation results. Then press **Unlock final test**.

The lab reveals the purple test examples and computes the selected model's **test RMSE**. The degree selection is locked for that run, because changing your model *after* seeing its test score would turn the test set into another validation set.

Compare validation RMSE with test RMSE. They won't necessarily match: both are estimates based on finite samples. A single small synthetic experiment does not guarantee how a real-world model will perform.

## A Critical Distinction: Selecting vs. Evaluating

**Parameter fitting** uses training data. **Hyperparameter selection** compares choices using validation data. **Final evaluation** estimates generalization with untouched test data.

These stages should be treated as different activities, even if the same program performs all three.

A subtle but important detail: **you can overfit the validation set too**. Suppose you try hundreds of model variants, choosing the one that happens to score best on a particular validation sample. Even without using validation examples to fit coefficients, your repeated choices can exploit random quirks in that sample. The final test helps detect overoptimistic selection.

### What about cross-validation?

With limited data, a single validation split can be noisy. **Cross-validation** repeats training and evaluation using different held-out folds, giving a more stable basis for model selection. Géron discusses this in Chapter 2 (pp. 88–97). We'll cover it more fully when we reach *Model Selection and Hyperparameter Tuning* in Module 4.

For now, the three-way holdout is the simplest way to learn the separation of roles.

## Guided Practice

Use the experiment to investigate these questions before taking the quiz:

1. With 60 / 20 / 20, how many observations go into each group? Why are there no purple test points initially?
2. For the medium-noise dataset, is the lowest training RMSE always associated with the lowest validation RMSE?
3. Increase **Noise level**. What changes in the training and validation RMSE values?
4. Enable validation leakage and retrain. Why is the displayed validation error no longer trustworthy as an independent score?
5. Finalize a model. Why should you *not* change the degree repeatedly in response to test scores?

**Experiment limitations:** The data is fictional, observations are independent by construction, and the models use straightforward polynomial least-squares regression. For real data involving people, patients, groups, locations, or time series, a random row split may be inappropriate. Group-aware or chronological splits may be necessary to prevent leakage. The lab is a teaching model, not a production evaluation pipeline.

## Key Takeaways

- The **training set** fits model parameters.
- The **validation set** helps choose model designs and hyperparameters.
- The **test set** estimates how the final, chosen model generalizes.
- **Data leakage** invalidates an evaluation by allowing information from a holdout set to influence training inappropriately.
- **Small error on training data** does not guarantee accurate predictions on unseen examples.
