## Predict With More Than One Input

In <Link href="/learn/ml/linear-regression">Lesson 08 — Linear Regression</Link>, we used a single input feature to predict a numerical value. But most useful predictions involve **several pieces of information at the same time**.

Imagine estimating an apartment's price. Floor area tells us something, but two equally sized apartments may have different prices if one is in a newer building. We might use **floor area** and **building age** together rather than expecting one feature to explain everything.

This is **multiple linear regression**: a linear model with **two or more input features**, predicting one numerical target. The word *multiple* refers to the inputs, not the number of outputs. Our example is entirely fictional, and the resulting prices are **not real estate advice**.

This lesson develops the multi-feature model, interprets its coefficients, shows how ordinary least squares fits them, and examines correlated features and omitted variables. The mathematical foundation follows **Aurélien Géron, *Hands-On Machine Learning*, 3rd edition**, Chapter 4, “Linear Regression” (pp. 132–137).

## Learning Objectives

By the end, you will be able to:

1. Write a linear regression equation with multiple input features.
2. Interpret each coefficient **while the other features are held fixed**.
3. Calculate a prediction, a residual, and the mean squared error.
4. Understand how a *design matrix* represents the full dataset.
5. Fit coefficients by minimizing squared residuals, and recognize when a unique solution may not exist.
6. Compare a model with one feature against a model with two features.
7. Evaluate predictions on a **held-out dataset** without using those samples to fit the coefficients.

## 1. The Multiple Linear Regression Model

For two inputs, the model is:

$$
\hat{y}=\theta_0+\theta_1x_1+\theta_2x_2
$$

For our fictional apartments:

| Symbol | What it represents | Unit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $x_1$ | Floor area | Square metres ($\mathrm{m}^2$) |
| $x_2$ | Building age | Years |
| $y$ | Observed apartment price | Thousands of AZN |
| $\hat{y}$ | Model-predicted price | Thousands of AZN |
| $\theta_0$ | Intercept | Thousands of AZN |
| $\theta_1$ | Area coefficient | Thousands of AZN per $\mathrm{m}^2$ |
| $\theta_2$ | Age coefficient | Thousands of AZN per year |

The intercept is the prediction when **every feature equals zero**. It is part of the mathematical model, but may have no practical interpretation if such a property is outside the observed data.

### Worked example — Predict an apartment price

Suppose a model has been fitted with the following coefficients:

$$
\hat{y}=25+2x_1-0.5x_2
$$

Consider an apartment with **80 m²** of area in a **10-year-old building**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{y}&=25+2(80)-0.5(10)\\
&=25+160-5\\
&=180
\end{aligned}
$$

The prediction is **180 thousand AZN**, or **180,000 AZN**.

Notice that each feature contributes independently to the *formula*. That does **not** mean the features are statistically independent in the dataset.

## 2. Interpret the Coefficients Correctly

The area coefficient $\theta_1=2$ says that **if we compare two inputs with the same age**, increasing floor area by one square metre increases this model's prediction by 2 thousand AZN.

The age coefficient $\theta_2=-0.5$ says that **if area is fixed**, increasing building age by one year decreases the model's prediction by 0.5 thousand AZN.

For example, increasing age from 10 to 15 years, with area unchanged, changes the model's prediction by:

$$
\Delta\hat{y}=(-0.5)(15-10)=-2.5
$$

That is a decrease of **2.5 thousand AZN**. We do not have to refit the model to calculate the effect of changing its inputs.

**Critical distinction:** A regression coefficient describes an **association in the fitted model, conditional on its other features**. It does *not* prove that making a building older would cause its market price to fall by exactly that amount. Location, renovations, building quality, and many other unmeasured factors may affect the relationship.

Also remember that coefficients have **units**. Comparing the raw magnitude of an area coefficient with that of an age coefficient is not a reliable measure of feature importance: a one-year change and a one-square-metre change are different quantities.

## 3. From Individual Examples to a Design Matrix

A dataset with $m$ training examples and $n$ features can be represented by an input matrix $X$. For each example, we place a leading **1** before its feature values so that the intercept is included in matrix multiplication.

For three hypothetical apartments, the design matrix could be:

$$
X=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 60 & 5\\
1 & 80 & 10\\
1 & 100 & 20
\end{bmatrix}
\qquad
\boldsymbol\theta=\begin{bmatrix}\theta_0\\\theta_1\\\theta_2\end{bmatrix}
$$

Then the vector of model predictions is:

$$
\hat{\mathbf y}=X\boldsymbol\theta
$$

Every **row** represents an apartment. Every **feature column** contains a measurement. Every coefficient is shared across all apartments; we do not invent a separate model for each row.

In general, the prediction for an example is a dot product:

$$
\hat{y}^{(i)}=\boldsymbol\theta^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x^{(i)}
$$

The extra constant feature $x_0=1$ multiplies the intercept. This compact vector notation becomes useful when working with many features and algorithms such as gradient descent or neural networks.

## 4. How the Model Learns the Coefficients

A training example contains an observed price $y^{(i)}$. Once we choose a model, it predicts $\hat y^{(i)}$. Their difference is the **residual**:

$$
r_i=y^{(i)}-\hat{y}^{(i)}
$$

The training objective is usually **mean squared error (MSE)**:

$$
\mathrm{MSE}(\boldsymbol\theta)
=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(y^{(i)}-\boldsymbol\theta^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x^{(i)}\right)^2
$$

The model should find coefficients that make this quantity as small as possible on the **training rows**. This is the same squared-error principle introduced in <Link href="/learn/ml/loss-functions">Lesson 09 — Loss Functions</Link>. The difference is that we now adjust *three* parameters instead of two.

### Ordinary least squares (OLS)

For a full-column-rank design matrix, one mathematical expression for the minimizing coefficients is the **Normal Equation**:

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}=(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y
$$

Here $X^{\mathsf T}$ is the transpose of $X$, and $^{-1}$ denotes a matrix inverse. The expression is **not valid when $X^{\mathsf T}X$ is singular**. Real software often solves least-squares problems using more robust factorizations such as QR or singular value decomposition (SVD), rather than explicitly computing the inverse.

Our browser lab solves the small least-squares normal system with **partial-pivoting elimination** and reports when a unique solution cannot be identified. It does not call a machine-learning API, and it does not claim that an ill-conditioned system is solved as robustly as a mature numerical library.

Another approach is to start with initial coefficients and update them repeatedly using **gradient descent**:

$$
\boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta-\eta\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\mathrm{MSE}(\boldsymbol\theta)
$$

We studied that idea in <Link href="/learn/ml/gradient-descent">Lesson 10 — Gradient Descent</Link>. Both OLS and gradient descent can seek the minimum of the **same linear-regression squared-error objective**, but they use different numerical procedures.

## 5. Why Adding a Feature Can Change Other Coefficients

A coefficient estimated with only area in the model answers a different question from an area coefficient estimated while controlling for building age.

Suppose larger apartments in your sample also tend to be **newer**. If you omit building age, the area-only model may partly absorb the information carried by age. When age is added, both coefficients may shift because OLS is solving a different optimization problem.

This is the idea behind **omitted-variable effects**. Removing a feature does not simply delete its term from an already fitted model: we need to **fit the remaining coefficients again**.

### Correlated features and multicollinearity

If area and age are strongly related, the dataset contains less independent information about their separate associations with price. Two distinct coefficient combinations might produce very similar fitted prices.

When the columns are perfectly linearly dependent (once the intercept is included), the data do not determine a unique OLS coefficient vector. When they are **nearly** dependent, estimates can become highly sensitive to small data changes. This is called **multicollinearity**.

In the lab, choose **Correlated features**, include both inputs, and compare the resulting coefficients with those from the **Mixed area + age** dataset. The warnings and correlation statistic help you recognize when interpretation becomes difficult. The correlation statistic does not itself prove that the coefficients are inaccurate, but it is a useful diagnostic.

## 6. Training Performance Is Not Generalization

A flexible or carefully fitted model can make its training error small without necessarily performing well on new apartments. This is why <Link href="/learn/ml/training-validation-and-testing">Lesson 05 — Training, Validation, and Testing</Link> kept evaluation examples separate from training.

Our experiment has a fixed, disclosed split of **14 training apartments** and **4 holdout apartments**. The fitting calculation sees only the training rows. The holdout observations are used only to compute separate predictions and errors, never to calculate OLS coefficients.

**RMSE** has the same units as the target:

$$
\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m\left(y^{(i)}-\hat y^{(i)}\right)^2}
$$

The plot places **observed prices on the horizontal axis** and **predicted prices on the vertical axis**. A point exactly on the diagonal is predicted perfectly; its vertical distance from that diagonal reflects its residual.

Both displayed RMSE values are **descriptive results on a small synthetic dataset**, not evidence of real-world apartment-price accuracy. Avoid tuning your model by repeatedly selecting whichever option performs best on the holdout; doing that turns the holdout into a validation set and weakens its independence.

## 7. When Multiple Linear Regression Is Useful—and When It Isn't

Multiple regression works well as an **interpretable baseline** when relationships are approximately additive and linear in the chosen parameters. It can handle many numeric features and can also use encoded categorical inputs.

But adding more columns does not guarantee better generalization. Potential limitations include:

- Important predictors are missing or measured poorly.
- The relationship is curved, changes across groups, or depends on **interactions** between features.
- Training observations are too few for the number of parameters.
- Predictors are highly correlated, making individual coefficients unstable.
- Outliers or distribution shifts make training results misleading.

We'll explore curved terms in **Lesson 12 — Polynomial Regression**, and coefficient penalties in **Lesson 13 — Regularization**. In practice, you should also consider a validation strategy, feature transformations, and the real purpose of the prediction before using this model to support a decision.

## Key Takeaways

- **Multiple linear regression** predicts one numerical target using two or more inputs and shared coefficients.
- A feature coefficient represents the model's predicted change **when the other included features are held fixed**.
- **OLS** chooses coefficients that minimize the training sum of squared residuals; it can be computed directly or approached with iterative optimization.
- **Correlated predictors** may lead to unstable, difficult-to-interpret individual coefficients; perfect column dependence prevents a unique unregularized solution.
- Removing a feature requires **refitting** the remaining coefficients, and can change their values.
- **Training** and **holdout** errors answer different questions. Good training fit does not guarantee generalization or causality.
